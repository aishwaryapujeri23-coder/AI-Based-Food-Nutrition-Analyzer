Nutrition Guidance for PCOS / PCOD

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), sometimes called PCOD, is commonly
associated with insulin resistance, which can make blood sugar management
and weight management more challenging. A lower glycemic-load, anti-
inflammatory eating pattern is often recommended alongside regular
physical activity.

Key principles:
- Favor low glycemic-index carbohydrates (whole grains, legumes, most
  vegetables) over refined carbohydrates, since these cause smaller
  insulin spikes.
- Pair carbohydrates with protein or healthy fat at each meal to further
  reduce insulin spikes and improve satiety.
- Include healthy fats such as nuts, seeds, olive oil, and avocado, which
  support hormonal health and help slow digestion.
- High-fiber vegetables help with both blood sugar control and digestive
  health.
- Anti-inflammatory spices such as turmeric and cinnamon are sometimes
  included as complementary additions, though evidence varies and they are
  not a replacement for a balanced diet.
- Regular meal timing helps some people with PCOS manage energy levels and
  insulin response more predictably than skipping meals or eating
  irregularly.

Foods and habits to limit:
- Refined sugar, sweets, and sugary beverages.
- White bread, maida-based foods, and other highly refined carbohydrates.
- Highly processed foods in general.
- For some individuals, excess dairy is associated with symptom flares,
  though this varies from person to person and is not universal.

Practical tips:
- Combining dietary changes with regular physical activity (both cardio
  and strength training) tends to produce better results for insulin
  sensitivity than diet alone.
- Weight management, even modest reductions of 5-10% of body weight, can
  meaningfully improve PCOS symptoms for many people who are above their
  ideal weight range.
- Since PCOS presentations vary widely, working with a doctor or
  dietitian to tailor the approach (including checking for insulin
  resistance markers) is valuable.

This information is educational and general in nature and does not
replace individualized care from a doctor, endocrinologist, or dietitian.
